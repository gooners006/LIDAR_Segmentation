\chapter*{Abstract}
\label{ch:abstract}

Automotive LiDAR sees every car from one side: the occluded surface is missing, and
point-cloud completion aims to restore it. On real data, however, that completion cannot
be measured. No clean reference shape --- no CAD model or mesh --- exists for a real car,
and the natural surrogate, reference-based Chamfer distance against an accumulated
multi-frame cloud, is invalid: because the accumulation is itself occluded on the same
side, the raw partial input scores \emph{best}, ranking a network that does nothing above
one that genuinely completes. Completion quality on real LiDAR thus had no valid metric.

The central contribution of this thesis is such a metric. It defines and validates a
leakage-free, donor-frame occluded-side coverage metric that scores how much of the
surface seen only in \emph{other} frames of the same static car a completion recovers,
guarded against surface hallucinated outside the car's box and benchmarked against a
symmetry-mirror baseline that adds surface with no learned parameters. The metric passes
a four-part validation battery before it is used. Applied to real sequence-08 cars, it
shows that a PCN completion network recovers genuinely unseen surface well above the
mirror baseline and improves amodal bounding boxes on static, gate-passed cars --- a
benefit that partially replicates under a pre-registered held-out test.

The metric is developed inside an interpretable, near-annotation-free pipeline that uses
classical geometry where it is reliable (ground removal and HDBSCAN density clustering)
and lightweight learned models only where discrimination is necessary (a binary car
classifier and the completion network). Characterising this pipeline yields two
diagnostic findings. Detection is high-precision (\num{0.905}) but structurally
recall-limited (\num{0.730} at a point-IoU of \num{0.3}, on cars with enough returns to be
scorable; validated across all eleven labelled sequences to rule out model-selection
bias), a shortfall root-caused to density clustering fragmenting single vehicles that
multiple repair strategies failed to overcome; and synthetic classifier pretraining is
redundant once real training clusters are plentiful. The same clustering step also governs
completion: an eleven-sequence ablation shows that input quality, not the choice of
completion model, decides whether completion is usable at all.

The thesis's contributions are thus methodological and diagnostic: a validated,
leakage-free metric for completion quality on real LiDAR; a root-cause account of why the
classical clustering detector is recall-limited; and evidence that synthetic pretraining
is redundant at scale.
