\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Rationale}
\label{sec:intro-background}

Autonomous driving depends on a perception system that can recover, several times a
second, the three-dimensional layout of the scene around the vehicle: where the other
road users are, how large they are, and which way they face. LiDAR is central to this
task. It measures range directly, returning an unordered cloud of over a hundred
thousand points per scan, largely invariant to lighting and carrying the metric depth
that motion planning needs. The benchmarks that opened the field were built around it:
the KITTI suite~\cite{geiger2012we} paired a scanning LiDAR with calibrated cameras,
and its point-wise successor SemanticKITTI~\cite{Behley_2019_ICCV} turned the same
sequences into the dense segmentation benchmark much of the field is now measured
against.

The dominant paradigm is to train a deep network end to end on annotated scans:
point-based segmentation networks~\cite{Qi_2017_CVPR,NIPS2017_d8bf84be,Hu_2020_CVPR},
projection- and voxel-based models~\cite{10.1109/ICRA.2018.8462926,milioto2019rangenet++,zhu2021cylindrical},
single-pass object detectors~\cite{lang2019pointpillars,yin2021centerpoint}, and
panoptic models~\cite{zhou2021panopticpolarnet,hong2021dsnet}. These define the state
of the art in accuracy, at two primary costs. The first is supervision: the
results rest on dense, point-wise labels, and the annotation behind a benchmark like
SemanticKITTI, a class on every one of billions of points, is prohibitively expensive for
most groups and most new datasets. The second is opacity: an end-to-end network spreads
each decision across millions of weights, making it difficult to isolate or debug
missed detections.

In contrast to end-to-end architectures, this thesis investigates the limits of a modular
perception pipeline that requires zero additional manual annotation. By designing a system
where classical geometric methods handle structural processing, while lightweight learned
models are reserved for discriminative tasks, it pursues an approach to LiDAR vehicle
detection that has drawn renewed interest~\cite{lim2025longrange}.
Clustering proposes candidate objects, a small binary car/not-car
classifier keeps the vehicles, and a PCN network completes each detected car. Every
stage is separately inspectable. The classifier is trained on supervision auto-mined
from existing semantic datasets, and the completion network on synthetic shapes alone. Chapter~\ref{ch:pipeline} gives the full pipeline
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\section{Completing Occluded Vehicles on Real LiDAR}
\label{sec:intro-challenges}

The completion stage of this framework introduces a more complex challenge. Completing a
partially observed car, recovering the far side the sensor cannot reach, is attractive
for downstream box estimation, and networks such as
PCN~\cite{yuan2019pcnpointcompletionnetwork} are well studied on synthetic benchmarks.
Carrying them onto real LiDAR raises two coupled difficulties. The first is training
without paired supervision: no dataset holds a real occluded car beside its complete
shape, so the network is trained on synthetic CAD partials and must transfer to real
scans it never saw during training. The second is evaluation: on real LiDAR there is no
clean ground-truth complete shape to score against, and the obvious substitute of
accumulating the same static car across many frames is itself occluded on the same side,
so reference-free fidelity metrics reward doing nothing at all. This thesis therefore asks
not whether completion lowers a distance to a nonexistent reference, but whether it
produces shapes that read as genuine cars on real data, judged by evidence independent of
any self-made reference.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-objectives}

This thesis pursues three objectives, spanning the detection front end, the completion
back end, and the evaluation that ties them together.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item To investigate the viability of a modular, geometry-first approach for LiDAR vehicle detection as a transparent alternative to opaque, data-hungry end-to-end networks.
  \item To establish a robust methodology for evaluating point cloud completion on real-world LiDAR data, where complete ground-truth shapes are inherently unavailable.
  \item To determine the extent to which synthetic-only training and auto-mined supervision can generalise to real LiDAR occlusions without requiring manually annotated real-world counterparts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

The thesis makes two contributions, together with the reproducibility infrastructure
that supports them.

\begin{itemize}
  \item This research introduces an end-to-end modular detection and completion framework for occluded vehicles. By integrating classical geometric processing with lightweight learned discriminative models, the pipeline achieves strong detection performance on real LiDAR while remaining fully inspectable and reproducible at every stage.

  \item The proposed framework requires zero additional manual annotation by utilising auto-mined supervision and synthetic training. The classifier's training data is automatically mined from existing semantic datasets, while the completion network is trained exclusively on synthetic CAD shapes, allowing the entire pipeline to generalise to new real-world sequences without the need for fresh bounding box annotations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Organisation}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

The remainder of the thesis is organised into four chapters.

Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: Literature Review \& Theoretical Background
places the pipeline against the deep learning segmentation and point cloud completion
literature, provides a comparative positioning table, and establishes the formal
notation and definitions for the operators the pipeline is built from.

Chapter~\ref{ch:pipeline}: Proposed Methodology describes every stage of the
framework: ground removal, clustering, the binary classifier and its training, the
centroid tracker, and the point cloud completion network. Each stage is given in its
configured, instantiated form, with its input-to-output contract, cross-referencing the
general operator definitions in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}: Experimental Evaluation evaluates the framework
end to end. It fixes the detection protocol and reports the per stage and
cross-validated detection results, the ablations, the distance breakdown, and the
runtime. It then traces the recall shortfall back to clustering fragmentation and
reports the chain of negative repair strategies, which also bridges to completion.
Finally, it evaluates the completion stage through synthetic Chamfer and F-score, the
independent-classifier plausibility of the completed real clouds, and reference-free
fidelity reported with its caveat.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}: Conclusion and Future Work draws together the
principal outcomes, acknowledges the study's limitations, and outlines directions for
future research.
