	\section{Introduction}
	
	The rapid advancement of autonomous driving and robotic navigation has placed
	three-dimensional scene understanding at the forefront of modern perception
	research. LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) sensors have become a cornerstone
	of outdoor 3D perception pipelines, providing precise geometric measurements of
	the surrounding environment \cite{Behley_2019_ICCV, 6248074}. However, due to
	physical constraints such as limited angular resolution and beam count, raw
	LiDAR scans produce inherently sparse point clouds that capture only a fraction
	of the true scene geometry. This sparsity is further exacerbated at longer
	ranges and in the presence of occlusions caused by dynamic objects such as
	vehicles and pedestrians \cite{10469138}.
	
	To bridge the gap between sparse sensor observations and the dense 3D
	representations required for safe navigation, the task of Semantic Scene
	Completion (SSC) has emerged as a critical research direction. SSC aims to
	jointly predict the occupancy and semantic class of every voxel in a defined 3D
	volume from partial observations \cite{Song_2017_CVPR}. While supervised
	approaches have achieved notable performance on established benchmarks
	\cite{Li_2023_CVPR, 9320442, Cao_2022_CVPR}, they are fundamentally dependent
	on dense, voxel-level ground-truth annotations. The acquisition of such
	annotations is prohibitively expensive and labor-intensive, requiring
	specialized equipment and extensive manual correction. This dependency on
	labeled data constitutes a significant bottleneck that limits the scalability
	and generalizability of current methods to new environments and sensor
	configurations.
	
	Unsupervised and self-supervised learning paradigms offer a principled
	alternative by leveraging geometric priors and structural regularities inherent
	in outdoor environments, such as ground planarity, surface continuity, and
	temporal consistency across sequential scans \cite{9812507, 8793637}. Despite
	their promise, the application of unsupervised methodologies to full-scale
	outdoor 3D scene completion remains underexplored. Existing unsupervised efforts
	have predominantly focused on object-level completion using synthetic datasets
	\cite{10959142, lee2025unsupervisedpointcloudcompletion}, and their extension to
	large-scale, real-world driving scenarios with complex occlusion patterns and
	dynamic content presents substantial open challenges.
	
	Consequently, the primary objective of this thesis is to investigate and develop
	an unsupervised learning framework for 3D scene reconstruction from sparse
	LiDAR point clouds. The proposed approach formulates the task as voxel-based
	occupancy prediction, leveraging geometric consistency losses and structural
	priors to train without ground-truth dense annotations. The framework will be
	evaluated on the SemanticKITTI Scene Completion benchmark
	\cite{Behley_2019_ICCV, 10802143}, enabling direct comparison with established
	supervised baselines and providing a quantitative assessment of the viability of
	unsupervised methods for this task.
	
	\section{Background}
	
	To establish a comprehensive foundation for this proposal, it is essential to
	examine the current state of research across three interconnected domains. This
	section outlines existing methodologies, recent advancements, and the critical
	challenges that motivate an unsupervised approach to 3D scene reconstruction
	from sparse LiDAR data.
	
	\subsection{LiDAR-Based Semantic Scene Completion}
	
	The task of Semantic Scene Completion (SSC) was formally introduced by Song et
	al. \cite{Song_2017_CVPR}, who proposed SSCNet to jointly infer the occupancy
	and semantic labels of a voxelized 3D volume from a single depth image. This
	formulation established the standard pipeline: given a partial observation of a
	scene, the model must predict the complete volumetric representation including
	regions that are unobserved or occluded. The task has since become a central
	benchmark for evaluating holistic 3D scene understanding in autonomous driving
	contexts \cite{10469138, 10802143}.
	
	Subsequent research has extended SSCNet along multiple axes. LMSCNet
	\cite{9320442} introduced a lightweight multi-scale architecture that
	significantly reduced computational cost while maintaining competitive
	accuracy, making real-time deployment more feasible. S3CNet
	\cite{pmlr-v155-cheng21a} addressed the challenge of sparse LiDAR inputs
	directly, designing a network that operates on raw point clouds rather than
	dense voxelized inputs. More recently, VoxFormer \cite{Li_2023_CVPR} applied
	the Transformer architecture to sparse voxel queries, achieving
	state-of-the-art results on the SemanticKITTI benchmark by decoupling geometry
	completion from semantic labeling. Camera-based approaches such as MonoScene
	\cite{Cao_2022_CVPR} have also demonstrated that monocular images alone can
	produce reasonable volumetric predictions, though with reduced geometric
	precision compared to LiDAR-based methods.
	
	Multi-modal fusion has emerged as another active direction. Lu et al.
	\cite{10613892} proposed a continuous voxelized fusion strategy that
	integrates LiDAR and camera features, while Xiao et al. \cite{10379527}
	introduced instance-level awareness into monocular SSC to improve the
	completion of individual objects. Sze and Kunze \cite{10588696} focused on
	real-time efficiency through memory-efficient sparse convolution, addressing
	the deployment constraints of autonomous vehicles. Yang et al. \cite{9636662} proposed a semantic segmentation-assisted
	approach that leverages 2D semantic predictions to guide 3D scene completion,
	demonstrating the benefit of auxiliary semantic cues for improving geometric
	reconstruction. Rist et al. \cite{9477025} explored deep implicit functions
	as an alternative to discrete voxel grids, enabling continuous geometric
	representations from LiDAR data.
	
	Despite this progress, a fundamental limitation persists across all the above
	methods: they rely on dense voxel-level ground-truth annotations for training.
	The SemanticKITTI Scene Completion benchmark \cite{Behley_2019_ICCV} provides
	such annotations by aggregating multiple LiDAR scans and projecting semantic
	labels into a voxel grid, but this process is sensor-specific, environment-
	specific, and does not scale to new domains without significant re-annotation
	effort. This dependency on supervised labels motivates the exploration of
	unsupervised alternatives.
	
	\subsection{Unsupervised and Self-Supervised 3D Learning}
	
	The prohibitive cost of obtaining dense 3D annotations has driven significant
	research interest toward learning paradigms that reduce or eliminate the need
	for ground-truth labels. This section examines three related but distinct
	threads: self-supervised depth completion, unsupervised point cloud completion,
	and self-supervised occupancy prediction.
	
	Self-supervised depth completion methods aim to produce dense depth maps from
	sparse LiDAR inputs combined with monocular camera images. Ma et al.
	\cite{8793637} introduced a foundational framework that trains a sparse-to-dense
	network using photometric consistency between the predicted dense depth and
	the observed camera image, requiring no ground-truth dense depth maps. Song et
	al. \cite{9525167} extended this paradigm by integrating visual-LiDAR odometry
	to enforce geometric consistency across consecutive frames. For extremely sparse
	inputs, Hu et al. \cite{10258421} demonstrated self-supervised depth completion
	from a single-line LiDAR, pushing the boundary of how little geometric
	supervision is required. Feng et al. \cite{pmlr-v164-feng22a} further advanced
	self-supervised monocular depth estimation by using sparse LiDAR measurements
	as a complementary training signal rather than as direct supervision. While
	these methods successfully densify depth representations, they operate in 2.5D
	(depth image space) rather than producing full volumetric 3D reconstructions,
	limiting their applicability to downstream tasks that require complete scene
	geometry.
	
	In the domain of unsupervised point cloud completion, recent work has focused
	on learning to reconstruct complete 3D shapes without access to ground-truth
	complete point clouds. Wu et al. \cite{10959142} proposed a multi-view
	adversarial learning framework that leverages rendered 2D projections from
	multiple viewpoints as a supervisory signal, enabling completion through
	cross-view geometric agreement. Lee et al.
	\cite{lee2025unsupervisedpointcloudcompletion} formulated the completion task
	as an unbalanced optimal transport problem, providing a principled mathematical
	framework for matching partial observations to complete shapes without
	paired training data. However, both approaches have been evaluated primarily on
	synthetic object-level datasets such as ShapeNet, and their scalability to
	large-scale outdoor driving environments with hundreds of dynamic objects and
	complex occlusion patterns remains undemonstrated.
	
	A more recent and directly relevant thread is self-supervised occupancy
	prediction. SelfOcc \cite{Huang_2024_CVPR} demonstrated that 3D occupancy
	representations can be learned from camera inputs alone using neural rendering
	as the supervisory signal, bypassing the need for voxel-level annotations
	entirely. OccFeat \cite{Sirko-Galouchenko_2024_CVPR} approached the problem
	from a pretraining perspective, learning occupancy features in a
	self-supervised manner to improve downstream Bird's Eye View segmentation.
	Occupancy-MAE \cite{10273603} applied masked autoencoder pretraining to
	large-scale LiDAR point clouds, learning occupancy representations by
	reconstructing randomly masked voxel regions. Lange et al.
	\cite{lange2025selfsupervisedmultifutureoccupancyforecasting} extended
	self-supervised occupancy learning to the temporal dimension, predicting
	multiple future occupancy states without ground-truth annotations. Vizzo et
	al. \cite{9812507}
	proposed a self-supervised scan completion method specifically designed for
	sparse outdoor LiDAR, exploiting temporal aggregation of sequential scans as a
	proxy for ground-truth dense geometry. While these approaches demonstrate the
	feasibility of reducing label dependency, they either focus on pretraining
	representations for supervised fine-tuning, or address partial aspects of the
	completion task rather than full unsupervised scene reconstruction from sparse
	LiDAR inputs.
	
	\subsection{Neural Scene Representations for Outdoor Environments}
	
	An alternative paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction has emerged through neural
	scene representations, which learn continuous implicit or explicit functions to
	model geometry and appearance. These methods are inherently self-supervised in
	their training procedure, as they optimize scene representations through
	differentiable rendering losses without requiring 3D ground-truth annotations.
	
	Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have been adapted to outdoor driving contexts
	by several recent works. Lightning NeRF \cite{10611130} introduced a hybrid
	representation combining explicit voxel grids with implicit neural features to
	accelerate both training and rendering for large-scale driving scenes. S-NeRF++
	\cite{10891659} extended the NeRF framework to support autonomous driving
	simulation, enabling both reconstruction of existing scenes and generation of
	novel traffic configurations. DriveEnv-NeRF
	\cite{shen2024driveenvnerfexplorationnerfbasedautonomous} explored the use of
	NeRF-based reconstructions as virtual environments for validating autonomous
	driving systems, demonstrating the practical utility of high-fidelity neural
	scene models. Shen et al. \cite{shen2024neuralrenderingbasedurban} provided a
	broader survey of neural rendering techniques applied to urban scene
	reconstruction, identifying both the potential and the current limitations of
	these approaches for autonomous driving applications.
	
	Despite their capacity to produce visually compelling reconstructions, neural
	scene representations face several fundamental limitations in the context of
	this thesis. First, most NeRF-based methods require per-scene optimization,
	meaning a separate model must be trained for each individual scene rather than
	learning a generalizable reconstruction function across scenes. This makes
	them impractical for real-time deployment or large-scale evaluation across
	diverse environments. Second, these methods are primarily designed for static
	scenes and struggle with dynamic objects such as moving vehicles and
	pedestrians, which are prevalent in driving scenarios. Third, their output is a
	continuous radiance or density field rather than a discrete voxel occupancy
	grid, making direct comparison against established SSC benchmarks such as
	SemanticKITTI non-trivial without additional post-processing steps. For these
	reasons, neural scene representations, while promising for visualization and
	simulation tasks, are not directly suited to the structured evaluation framework
	of semantic scene completion benchmarks, and their application to unsupervised
	volumetric reconstruction from sparse LiDAR remains an open research direction.
	
	\subsection{Geometric Distance Metrics for 3D Reconstruction}
	
	Unsupervised 3D reconstruction methods depend critically on the choice of
	geometric loss function, as no ground-truth dense geometry is available during
	training. The loss must quantify the discrepancy between a predicted 3D
	structure and an observed partial measurement, and its properties directly
	influence training stability, convergence behavior, and the quality of
	reconstructed outputs. This section reviews the principal distance metrics
	used in point cloud and voxel-based reconstruction, with emphasis on recent
	variants that address known failure modes of the standard formulation.
	
	The Chamfer Distance (CD) is the most widely adopted metric for evaluating
	and training point cloud reconstruction models. Given a predicted point set
	$P$ and a reference point set $Q$, the symmetric Chamfer Distance is defined
	as:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\text{CD}(P, Q) = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{p \in P} \min_{q \in Q} \| p - q \|_2^2
		\;+\; \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \min_{p \in P} \| q - p \|_2^2
	\end{equation}
	
	The first term penalizes predicted points that lack nearby counterparts in the
	reference (accuracy), while the second penalizes reference points not covered
	by the prediction (completeness). Despite its simplicity and differentiability,
	the standard CD suffers from well-documented pathological behaviors. Wu et al.
	\cite{wu2021densityawarechamferdistancecomprehensive} demonstrated that CD is
	insensitive to local density variations: a prediction that clusters points
	densely in some regions while leaving others uncovered can achieve a low CD
	score, producing visually implausible reconstructions. To address this, they
	proposed Density-aware Chamfer Distance (DCD), which incorporates a
	density-penalizing term that encourages uniform coverage of the target surface.
	
	Several subsequent works have proposed alternative modifications to the CD
	formulation. Lin et al. \cite{Lin_2023_ICCV} introduced Hyperbolic Chamfer
	Distance, which embeds point representations in hyperbolic space to better
	capture hierarchical geometric structures, achieving improved completion
	quality on objects with complex topology. The same research group later
	proposed InfoCD \cite{NEURIPS2023_f2ea1943}, a contrastive formulation that
	reinterprets the completion loss through the lens of mutual information
	maximization, providing stronger gradients for training and reducing
	sensitivity to outliers. Huang et al. \cite{HUANG202443} proposed Learnable
	Chamfer Distance, which parameterizes the distance function itself as a
	trainable module, allowing the loss to adapt to the specific geometric
	characteristics of the reconstruction task during training. Lu et al.
	\cite{9785649} developed Oriented and Directional Chamfer Distance variants
	that incorporate surface normal information into the distance computation,
	improving reconstruction accuracy for objects with thin structures and sharp
	edges where standard Euclidean nearest-neighbor matching is ambiguous.
	
	In the voxelized setting relevant to semantic scene completion, the
	reconstruction objective is typically formulated as per-voxel binary
	classification (occupied vs. free) rather than point-to-point distance
	minimization. Binary cross-entropy and its class-balanced variants are the
	standard losses for supervised SSC. For unsupervised training, however, the
	loss must be derived from geometric consistency between predicted voxel
	occupancy and observed point clouds or multi-frame aggregations, making the
	choice and adaptation of geometric distance metrics a central design decision
	in the proposed framework.
	
	\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}
	
	The preceding literature review reveals a clear gap at the intersection of
	three research threads. Supervised semantic scene completion methods
	(Section~2.1) achieve strong performance but are fundamentally constrained by
	the requirement for dense voxel-level annotations that are expensive to
	acquire and do not transfer across sensor configurations or environments.
	Unsupervised and self-supervised 3D learning approaches (Section~2.2) have
	demonstrated that meaningful geometric representations can be learned without
	dense labels, but existing work has either focused on 2.5D depth completion,
	object-level point cloud completion on synthetic data, or pretraining
	representations intended for subsequent supervised fine-tuning. Neural scene
	representations (Section~2.3) offer an alternative reconstruction paradigm but
	require per-scene optimization and produce continuous fields that are
	incompatible with the discrete voxel evaluation framework of established SSC
	benchmarks. Furthermore, the geometric distance metrics reviewed in
	Section~2.4 exhibit pathological behaviors under standard formulations,
	adding complexity to the design of unsupervised training objectives that must
	operate without direct supervision.
	
	To date, no existing method has demonstrated fully unsupervised scene
	completion from sparse LiDAR point clouds at the scale and evaluation
	rigor of standard benchmarks such as SemanticKITTI. The specific open
	challenges that this thesis aims to address are:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Scalability of unsupervised completion to full outdoor scenes.}
		Existing unsupervised point cloud completion methods
		\cite{10959142, lee2025unsupervisedpointcloudcompletion} operate on
		isolated synthetic objects. Extending these to large-scale outdoor
		environments with hundreds of objects, complex occlusion patterns, and
		significant depth variation is non-trivial.
		\item \textbf{Geometric supervision without dense ground truth.} The
		unsupervised training signal must be derived entirely from the sparse
		observations themselves. Temporal aggregation of sequential LiDAR scans
		\cite{9812507} provides a proxy for denser geometry, but designing loss
		functions that effectively exploit this signal for voxel-level occupancy
		prediction requires careful formulation.
		\item \textbf{Evaluation against established supervised baselines.} The
		viability of unsupervised methods must be assessed quantitatively on the
		same benchmarks used by supervised approaches. Understanding the
		performance gap and identifying where unsupervised methods succeed or
		fail relative to their supervised counterparts is essential for guiding
		future research.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	Formally, given a sparse LiDAR point cloud $\mathcal{X}_t \subset
	\mathbb{R}^3$ captured at time $t$, and a sequence of temporally adjacent
	scans $\{\mathcal{X}_{t-k}, \ldots, \mathcal{X}_{t+k}\}$ with known ego-motion
	transformations, the objective is to learn a function
	$f_\theta : \mathcal{X}_t \rightarrow \hat{\mathcal{V}} \in \{0, 1\}^{H
		\times W \times D}$ that predicts the binary occupancy of each voxel in a
	predefined 3D volume, \textit{without} access to ground-truth voxel labels
	$\mathcal{V}^*$ during training.
	
	\section{Proposed Methodology}
	
	This section presents the proposed unsupervised framework for 3D scene
	reconstruction from sparse LiDAR. The method consists of three principal
	components: a voxel-based encoder--decoder network for occupancy prediction,
	a temporal aggregation module that constructs pseudo-dense supervision from
	sequential scans, and a set of geometric consistency losses that provide the
	unsupervised training signal.
	
	\subsection{Framework Overview}
	
	The overall pipeline operates as follows. At training time, a sparse LiDAR
	scan $\mathcal{X}_t$ is voxelized into a binary input tensor
	$\mathcal{V}_{\text{in}} \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W \times D}$, where each
	voxel is marked as occupied if it contains at least one LiDAR return point.
	This sparse input is processed by a 3D encoder--decoder network that produces
	a dense occupancy probability map $\hat{\mathcal{V}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W
		\times D}$. Concurrently, the temporal aggregation module transforms and
	merges neighboring scans $\{\mathcal{X}_{t-k}, \ldots, \mathcal{X}_{t+k}\}$
	into the coordinate frame of $\mathcal{X}_t$ using ego-motion estimates,
	producing a pseudo-dense reference volume $\mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}$. The
	network parameters $\theta$ are optimized by minimizing a composite loss that
	measures geometric consistency between $\hat{\mathcal{V}}$ and
	$\mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}$, supplemented by structural regularization priors.
	At inference time, only the single-scan input path is used; the temporal
	aggregation module is not required.
	
	\subsection{Voxel Encoder--Decoder Network}
	
	The encoder--decoder architecture follows the established U-Net paradigm
	adapted for 3D sparse convolution, drawing on design principles from LMSCNet
	\cite{9320442} and S3CNet \cite{pmlr-v155-cheng21a}. The encoder applies a
	series of sparse 3D convolutional blocks with residual connections and
	progressive downsampling to extract multi-scale geometric features from the
	sparse input volume. Skip connections transmit high-resolution features to
	the decoder, which applies transposed sparse convolutions to upsample the
	representation back to the original voxel resolution. The final output layer
	applies a sigmoid activation to produce per-voxel occupancy probabilities.
	
	Sparse convolution is essential for computational efficiency, as the vast
	majority of voxels in outdoor LiDAR volumes are unoccupied. By operating only
	on non-empty voxels and their neighborhoods, the network avoids the cubic
	memory and computation costs of dense 3D convolution, following the
	efficiency principles demonstrated by Sze and Kunze \cite{10588696}.
	
	\subsection{Temporal Aggregation for Pseudo-Dense Supervision}
	
	The core insight enabling unsupervised training is that while any individual
	LiDAR scan is sparse, the aggregation of multiple temporally adjacent scans
	observed from different ego-vehicle positions yields a substantially denser
	representation of the static scene geometry \cite{9812507}. This aggregated
	volume serves as a pseudo-ground-truth supervisory signal.
	
	Given a sequence of scans $\{\mathcal{X}_{t-k}, \ldots, \mathcal{X}_{t+k}\}$
	and the corresponding ego-motion transformations $\{T_{t-k \rightarrow t},
	\ldots, T_{t+k \rightarrow t}\}$ (obtained from LiDAR odometry or GPS/IMU),
	each neighboring scan is transformed into the coordinate frame of the
	reference scan $\mathcal{X}_t$ and voxelized. The union of all transformed
	voxel occupancies produces $\mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}$. To mitigate noise
	introduced by dynamic objects that move between frames, a statistical
	filtering step removes voxels with inconsistent occupancy across the temporal
	window: voxels occupied in fewer than a threshold fraction of contributing
	scans are discarded. This approach follows the principle established by
	Vizzo et al. \cite{9812507} and is analogous to the multi-scan aggregation
	used to construct the SemanticKITTI ground truth \cite{Behley_2019_ICCV},
	but applied automatically without manual annotation.
	
	\subsection{Unsupervised Loss Formulation}
	
	The training objective combines three complementary loss terms:
	
	\paragraph{Occupancy consistency loss.} The primary loss measures the
	agreement between predicted occupancy $\hat{\mathcal{V}}$ and the pseudo-dense
	reference $\mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}$ using binary cross-entropy applied only
	to voxels where $\mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}$ provides reliable information
	(i.e., voxels that are either consistently occupied or confirmed free by
	ray-casting through the aggregated scans):
	\begin{equation}
		\mathcal{L}_{\text{occ}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{M}}
		\left[ \mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}(v) \log \hat{\mathcal{V}}(v) +
		(1 - \mathcal{V}_{\text{agg}}(v)) \log (1 - \hat{\mathcal{V}}(v)) \right]
	\end{equation}
	where $\mathcal{M}$ denotes the set of voxels with reliable pseudo-labels.
	
	\paragraph{Geometric smoothness prior.} A total-variation regularizer
	encourages spatial coherence in the predicted occupancy, penalizing isolated
	spurious voxels and encouraging smooth surfaces:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}} = \sum_{v} \sum_{v' \in \mathcal{N}(v)}
		\left| \hat{\mathcal{V}}(v) - \hat{\mathcal{V}}(v') \right|
	\end{equation}
	where $\mathcal{N}(v)$ denotes the 6-connected spatial neighborhood of
	voxel $v$.
	
	\paragraph{Ground plane constraint.} Outdoor driving scenes exhibit a strong
	structural prior: the ground surface is approximately planar and should be
	consistently occupied in the lowest voxel layers. A constraint term encourages
	high occupancy probability for voxels at the estimated ground height, derived
	from a RANSAC plane fit to the lowest LiDAR returns:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathcal{L}_{\text{ground}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{G}}
		\log \hat{\mathcal{V}}(v)
	\end{equation}
	where $\mathcal{G}$ is the set of voxels on the estimated ground plane.
	
	The total loss is:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{occ}} + \lambda_s \mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}
		+ \lambda_g \mathcal{L}_{\text{ground}}
	\end{equation}
	where $\lambda_s$ and $\lambda_g$ are weighting hyperparameters to be
	determined through ablation.
	
	\section{Experimental Design}
	
	\subsection{Dataset and Benchmark}
	
	The proposed method will be evaluated on the SemanticKITTI Scene Completion
	benchmark \cite{Behley_2019_ICCV, 10802143}, which provides voxelized
	ground-truth occupancy and semantic labels for 22 driving sequences captured
	by a Velodyne HDL-64E LiDAR sensor. The standard split uses sequences 00--10
	for training (with sequence 08 reserved for validation) and sequences 11--21
	for testing. The completion volume is defined as $256 \times 256 \times 32$
	voxels at $0.2\,\text{m}$ resolution, covering $51.2\,\text{m} \times
	51.2\,\text{m} \times 6.4\,\text{m}$ in front of the ego-vehicle.
	Additionally, the Occ3D benchmark \cite{NEURIPS2023_cabfaeec} will be
	considered as a secondary evaluation to assess generalizability across
	different annotation pipelines and sensor configurations.
	
	\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
	
	Following standard SSC evaluation protocol, the primary metric for geometry
	completion (the focus of this thesis) is the Intersection over Union (IoU)
	computed over all occupied voxels regardless of semantic class:
	\begin{equation}
		\text{IoU} = \frac{|\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{occ}} \cap \mathcal{V}^*_{\text{occ}}|}
		{|\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{occ}} \cup \mathcal{V}^*_{\text{occ}}|}
	\end{equation}
	where $\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{occ}}$ and $\mathcal{V}^*_{\text{occ}}$
	denote the sets of voxels predicted and labeled as occupied, respectively.
	Precision and recall will also be reported to distinguish between accuracy
	and completeness of the reconstruction. The analysis will additionally
	examine performance stratified by distance from the ego-vehicle, as
	reconstruction difficulty increases with range due to increasing point cloud
	sparsity.
	
	\subsection{Baselines and Comparisons}
	
	The experimental comparison will include the following categories:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Supervised SSC methods} (upper-bound reference): LMSCNet
		\cite{9320442}, S3CNet \cite{pmlr-v155-cheng21a}, VoxFormer
		\cite{Li_2023_CVPR}, and MonoScene \cite{Cao_2022_CVPR}.
		\item \textbf{Self-supervised methods}: Vizzo et al. \cite{9812507}
		(self-supervised scan completion), Occupancy-MAE \cite{10273603}
		(self-supervised pretraining), and SelfOcc \cite{Huang_2024_CVPR}
		(self-supervised occupancy from cameras).
		\item \textbf{Ablated variants of the proposed method}: to isolate the
		contribution of each loss component ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{occ}}$,
		$\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ground}}$), the
		temporal aggregation window size $k$, and the dynamic object filtering
		threshold.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\section{Expected Contributions}
	
	This thesis is expected to make the following contributions:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item An unsupervised learning framework for voxel-based 3D scene
		reconstruction from sparse LiDAR that requires no dense ground-truth
		annotations, combining temporal aggregation with geometric consistency
		losses and structural priors.
		\item A systematic quantitative comparison between the proposed unsupervised
		approach and established supervised baselines on the SemanticKITTI Scene
		Completion benchmark, providing empirical evidence on the viability and
		current limitations of label-free methods for this task.
		\item An analysis of the conditions under which unsupervised reconstruction
		succeeds or fails (e.g., distance ranges, scene complexity, dynamic
		object density), informing future research directions in self-supervised
		3D perception.
	\end{enumerate}
